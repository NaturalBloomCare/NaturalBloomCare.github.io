Dry air, dry skin — caring for your skin through a Calgary winter

Gentle, everyday habits for skin that feels tight, itchy or flaky when the heat is on and the air is dry.

Written by the Bloom care team

When the furnace is running and the air outside is cold and dry, many people notice their skin change. Hands can feel tight after washing, shins can look flaky, and lips can crack. Calgary’s cold, dry winter air and indoor heating can make this a familiar part of the season.

This article shares general, everyday skin-care habits for dry winter air. It is not a treatment plan for any skin condition, and it does not replace an assessment if your skin is sore, cracked, very itchy or not improving.

Why winter air can dry out your skin

The outer layer of your skin helps hold water in. MyHealth.Alberta.ca notes that dry indoor air, low-humidity climates, indoor heating, and frequent or hot baths and showers can all dry the skin. As we age, the skin also makes less of the natural oil that helps it keep moisture.

Health Canada notes that indoor relative humidity below 30% can happen in winter. Low humidity may irritate the eyes, can lead to cracked skin, and adds to static “sparks” when you touch a door handle.

Gentle habits in the shower and bath

MyHealth.Alberta.ca suggests a few simple changes:

  • Keep showers and baths short, and use warm or lukewarm water rather than hot water, which takes off more of the skin’s natural oils.
  • Use a mild, gentle cleanser instead of harsh soap, and wash gently rather than scrubbing.
  • Pat your skin dry with a towel instead of rubbing.
  • Put on a moisturizer right away, within about three minutes, while your skin is still a little damp.

You do not need a long routine. Pairing moisturizer with something you already do — after a shower, after washing your hands, before bed — is often easier to keep up through a long winter.

Choosing and using a moisturizer

MyHealth.Alberta.ca notes that creams and ointments generally protect the skin better than thinner lotions, and that moisturizer can be used several times a day, as often as you need it. For very dry hands or feet, a thin layer of a thick ointment at bedtime under cotton gloves or socks can help keep moisture in overnight. A lip balm can help protect lips from cracking.

When you compare products, these label habits can help:

  • If your skin is easily irritated, fragrance-free products are often a gentler place to start.
  • Many moisturizers use plant-based ingredients such as colloidal oatmeal, shea butter or sunflower seed oil. A plant ingredient is not automatically gentler or safer, and any ingredient can irritate some people.
  • Try a new product on a small patch of skin first, such as the inner arm, and stop using it if you notice redness, burning or itching.
  • If a product says it treats a condition, look for an eight-digit Natural Product Number (NPN) or Drug Identification Number (DIN), read the directions and warnings, and ask a pharmacist whether it is right for you.

MyHealth.Alberta.ca also suggests using only a small amount of laundry detergent, skipping fabric softeners and dryer sheets, and wearing soft, loose clothing if scratchy fabrics bother your skin.

Bringing a little moisture back into the air

Health Canada suggests measuring indoor humidity with a hygrometer, an inexpensive device sold at hardware stores. If it reads below 30% in winter, a humidifier may help keep humidity around 30% to 35%. During very cold weather, many homes cannot hold much more than 30% without water collecting on windows. If you see a lot of condensation, turn the humidifier down, because ongoing dampness can lead to mould.

If you use a humidifier, follow the manufacturer’s cleaning instructions. For cool-mist humidifiers, Health Canada advises cleaning and disinfecting the water tank regularly, emptying it whenever the humidifier is turned off, and keeping the unit in a central spot rather than in the bedroom, especially overnight.

Protecting your skin outdoors

Cold wind can chap exposed skin quickly. Health Canada notes that the risk of windburn and frostbite rises at wind chill values below −27. On cold days, cover as much skin as you can with a hat, mittens or insulated gloves, and a scarf or face covering, and try to stay dry.

Know the early signs of cold injury. With frostnip, the skin may look yellowish or white but still feel soft, often with tingling or burning. With frostbite, the skin may look white and waxy, feel hard, and become numb. Do not rub or massage cold-injured skin. Warm it gradually, and get medical help for frostbite.

The sun still matters in winter. Environment and Climate Change Canada notes that fresh snow can reflect over 80% of the sun’s UV rays. Health Canada recommends a broad-spectrum sunscreen with SPF 30 or higher on uncovered skin, put on at least 15 minutes before you go out and reapplied at least every 2 hours, plus a lip balm with SPF. This matters most on bright, snowy days and on the ski hill, where higher elevation adds more UV.

Food and water as everyday support

No single food or drink fixes dry skin. Everyday eating and drinking habits are still part of looking after your whole body, including your skin. Canada’s food guide suggests making water your drink of choice and building a plate that is half vegetables and fruits, a quarter whole grains, and a quarter protein foods, choosing plant-based protein foods more often.

In winter, that might look like a warm bowl of lentil and vegetable soup, oatmeal topped with ground flaxseed and berries, or a grain bowl with roasted squash, chickpeas, greens and a sprinkle of sunflower seeds. Soups, fruit and vegetables also add to the fluid you take in each day. If you have been told to limit fluids for a health reason, follow your provider’s advice.

Supplements are sometimes marketed for skin. Talk with your healthcare provider or pharmacist before starting one, especially if you are pregnant, trying to become pregnant, breastfeeding, giving a product to a child, or taking other medicines.

When dry skin may be something more

Most winter dryness responds to gentle care, but not all dry, itchy skin is “just winter.” Skin conditions such as eczema or psoriasis can look like dry skin. Some medicines and other health conditions can also affect the skin. A Nurse Practitioner can assess your skin, help find what may be behind it, and talk with you about medical options when they are needed. Natural and lifestyle support, such as the habits in this article, can sit alongside that medical care, not replace it.

Avoid scratching, which can damage the skin. MyHealth.Alberta.ca suggests keeping nails trimmed and smooth, and asking a pharmacist or provider before using a non-prescription anti-itch product.

To take with you

Practical takeaways

  • Keep showers short and lukewarm, use a mild cleanser, pat dry, and moisturize within about three minutes.
  • Creams and ointments generally hold moisture in better than thin lotions. Patch-test new products, including plant-based ones.
  • Health Canada suggests indoor humidity around 30% to 35% in winter. Keep humidifiers clean and watch for condensation on windows.
  • Cover up on cold, windy days, and know the signs of frostnip and frostbite.
  • Snow reflects UV. Use a broad-spectrum SPF 30 or higher sunscreen and an SPF lip balm on bright winter days.
  • Water and a plant-forward plate support everyday health. No single food fixes dry skin.

Care in Alberta

When to see a healthcare provider

Contact a healthcare provider or call Health Link at 811 if your skin is cracked, bleeding or painful, if itching keeps you awake, if a rash spreads, or if your skin does not get better with gentle home care. Get care right away if you notice signs of infection, such as pain, warmth or swelling, red streaks, pus, or a fever.

Frostbite needs medical care. Call 911 or go to an emergency department if skin is white or waxy, hard and numb after cold exposure, or if someone is very cold, confused, or unusually drowsy. Bloom does not provide urgent or emergency care. If you or someone you know is thinking about suicide, call or text 988.
